# 9. Competitor Analysis: What Other Kids' Chess Apps Do Well (and Badly)

Research date: November 2026. Sources are listed at the end. Most information comes from app-store listings,
publisher pages and independent reviews (Common Sense Media, Chess.com blogs, parenting blogs). Store
listings are the developers' own claims, and prices change often, so the table below records **what each product does**,
not whether it is popular.

## 9.1 The landscape at a glance

| Product | Type | Ages | What it does best | Its weak spots (from reviews) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ChessKid** (Chess.com) | App + web platform | ~5–15 | Safety (locked-down chat, parent-linked accounts, activity reports, time limits), huge puzzle library, video lessons, school and club tools, used by chess-in-schools programmes | UI described as dated; free tier limited (about 3 puzzles a day); some kids outgrow it after the basics; lessons are video-heavy, which means watching rather than doing |
| **Chess Adventure for Kids** (ChessKid) | Quest app | ~4–10 | Fantasy quests, 40+ bosses, loot, puzzle duels; no ads | Reviewers say the bosses and puzzles are too easy; coins and in-app purchases; aimed at the youngest children |
| **Learn Chess with Dr. Wolf** (Chess.com) | Coaching app | Beginners of all ages | A coach who **talks you through your own games**, remembers your mistakes and reviews them; practice-based rather than video-based; voice | Coaching is behind a subscription after the trial; not designed specifically for young children |
| **Duolingo Chess** | Course in the Duolingo app | Beginners of all ages | Bite-sized lessons, placement check at the start, **about 75% puzzle-based**, a friendly coach character, mini-matches, game review, leaderboards, free | Reviewers call it shallow past the basics; wrong puzzles aren't reviewed later ("get one wrong and you move on"); no play against humans |
| **DragonBox Learn Chess** (formerly *Magnus' Kingdom of Chess*) | Adventure RPG | ~5+ | **The world itself moves like chess pieces**: characters walk the map the way their piece moves. Pieces are introduced one at a time, starting with the king, and the game builds up to a full game. One-off price, no ads | Leaves out castling and en passant; Common Sense says some battles are too complex for younger players and there is "little help", so an adult may be needed |
| **Story Time Chess** | Physical board game + storybook | 3+ | **A story and a character for every piece**, which makes the movement rules memorable; very strong with pre-schoolers | Not an app: it needs a parent to read the stories, so it isn't self-guided |
| **Fritz & Chesster** (ChessBase) | PC/browser game series | 6+ | Rules hidden inside **arcade mini-games** (the bishop learned by bouncing a ball, the rook by dodging spiders); a story with characters; **training rooms you must master before moving on** | Older production; distribution is patchy (some versions removed from stores) |
| **Dinosaur Chess** | App | ~5–10 | Clear modules (**Learn, Play, Fight, Progress**), 11 short lessons, 20+ mini-games, animated dinosaur opponents | Small amount of content; old technology (users report audio bugs) |
| **Chess Academy for Kids** (Geek Kids) | App | ~6+ | Tutorials, many settings to adjust difficulty and the computer's strength | Common Sense suggests parents read the tutorials *with* the child, so it isn't self-guided |
| **Lichess** (Learn, Practice, Coordinates) | Free web/app | All ages | Free, open source, ad-free; gamified **"Learn" rule lessons**, a coordinates trainer, practice positions, a "Class" feature for coaches; **a public-domain (CC0) puzzle database** | No child-specific safety features; not designed for young children |
| **Chessie** (2026) | Story-driven kids' app | Kids | Story-led; claims no ads, no tracking, no in-app purchases | New and unproven; claims not independently verified |

## 9.2 Patterns across the market

1. **The "too easy / too hard" split.** Reviews of almost every kids' app complain about one or the other.
   Adventure apps are "too simple" for kids who already know the rules; battle-style apps are "too complex"
   for 5-year-olds. **Nobody adjusts difficulty in both directions well.**
2. **Most "kids" apps still need an adult.** Common Sense Media repeatedly advises parents to sit with the child
   (DragonBox, Chess Academy for Kids), and Story Time Chess requires an adult to read. **A truly self-guided
   app for non-readers is a real gap.**
3. **Rules coverage is often incomplete.** Some apps skip castling and en passant. Kids then learn "wrong chess"
   and get confused in real games.
4. **Watching vs doing.** Video lessons (ChessKid, Chess.com) are less effective for young children than
   practice-based coaching (Dr. Wolf, Duolingo's puzzles).
5. **Mistakes are rarely recycled.** Duolingo Chess moves on after a wrong answer; only Dr. Wolf systematically remembers and reviews
   mistakes.
6. **Monetisation friction.** Daily puzzle caps, coins, and coaching behind paywalls interrupt learning flow.
7. **Safety is a selling point.** ChessKid's success with parents and schools comes largely from chat lock-down and
   parent controls.
8. **Graphics are mostly 2D and dated.** Only a few apps use 3D, and none make a real-board feel the centre of the experience.
   This is our clearest visual differentiator.

## 9.3 The good ideas we are adopting

| # | Idea | Seen in | How ChessMaster Academy uses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Moving across the world the way your piece moves** | DragonBox / Magnus' Kingdom | On the Adventure Map in Piece Valley, the child travels *as* the piece being learned: as a rook, they roll in straight lines between stepping stones; as a knight, they hop in L-shapes across the lily pads. Every bit of navigation is extra practice |
| 2 | **A story and character for every piece** | Story Time Chess | Each piece has a character and a short spoken story explaining *why* it moves that way (for example, Captain Rook's tower is on wheels and can only roll straight). Fully voiced, so no adult is needed to read it |
| 3 | **Rules hidden in arcade mini-games** | Fritz & Chesster | Mini-games such as Star Collector and Knight's Quest are fun first. A "training room" gate means the child masters a skill before moving on |
| 4 | **A coach who talks through your games and remembers your mistakes** | Dr. Wolf | Pip or Coach Nova gives live commentary in practice games ("Wait! Is your bishop safe there?"), offers a take-back with an explanation, and turns mistakes into "Oops Replay" puzzles that come back later. **Included free**, not behind a paywall |
| 5 | **Placement check at the start; mostly puzzles** | Duolingo Chess | A playful 3-minute placement quest for ages 8+ (and for younger kids who already play). About 70% of practice is interactive puzzles, not watching |
| 6 | **Short sessions and a clear daily goal** | Duolingo | "Today's adventure" = one lesson + one game + three review puzzles, about 10 minutes |
| 7 | **Clear four-part structure** | Dinosaur Chess | Home screen with just four doors: **Learn · Practise · Play · My Trophies** |
| 8 | **Animated opponents with personalities** | Dinosaur Chess, Chess Adventure | Bot characters (Bumble the Bear, Fizz the Fox…) with emotions and voice lines |
| 9 | **Safety and parent controls** | ChessKid | No free chat, preset emotes only, parent gate, activity reports, time limits |
| 10 | **School and coach tools** | ChessKid, Lichess Class | Classroom mode with join codes, assignments, a class heat-map |
| 11 | **Coordinates trainer** | Lichess | Coordinate Rush mini-game with the picture alphabet for young kids |
| 12 | **Free, public-domain puzzle data** | Lichess (CC0) | Puzzle Gym is seeded from the CC0 Lichess puzzle database, filtered for kids |
| 13 | **Difficulty settings** | Chess Academy for Kids | Older kids can choose bot strength; younger kids get automatic difficulty |
| 14 | **Game review highlighting key moments** | Duolingo, Chess.com | Coach's Corner: 3 key moments per game at most, explained in kid language |
| 15 | **Bosses as level gates** | Chess Adventure for Kids | Boss Battles at the end of each kingdom, but adaptive, so never "too easy" |

## 9.4 The mistakes we are avoiding

| Weakness seen in the market | Our answer |
|---|---|
| Needs an adult to read or explain | **Voice-first, no reading required for ages 5–7**, plus self-guided learning design (see [document 11](11-self-guided-learning.md)) |
| Too easy for kids who already know the rules | Placement quest + adaptive difficulty + a "Too easy? Skip ahead!" challenge on every lesson |
| Too hard for young children | Errorless mode, mini-boards, hint ladder, and the app automatically simplifies after repeated failure |
| Skips castling and en passant | 100% rules coverage, tracked in the rules coverage matrix (document 11, section 11.9) |
| Wrong answers forgotten | Spaced repetition of every concept and every mistake |
| Learning by watching videos | Learning by doing: every explanation is followed by an interaction within 20 seconds |
| Paywalls in the middle of learning (daily caps, coins) | No daily caps, no coins, no in-app currency. The full rules curriculum (Levels 1–3) is free |
| Dated 2D graphics | Premium 3D with a real-board feel, and a 2D option |
| Chat and stranger risks | No free chat; friend games only with invite codes and parent approval |

## 9.5 Our position in one sentence

> **The only kids' chess app where a 5-year-old who can't read can learn every rule of real chess on their own,
> on a beautiful 3D board, with a coach who listens, questions and adapts, and that keeps growing with them up to tournament level.**
